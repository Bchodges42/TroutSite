## node n-xvoeOU

The Hiwassee River is located in Polk County near the town of Reliance. It is one of the most unique tailwater trout fisheries in Tennessee. Water from Apalachia Dam in North Carolina is diverted through large pipes that tunnel through 8 miles of mountains to produce electricity at the Apalachia Powerhouse. The Hiwassee River provides 17 miles of trout water from Apalachia Powerhouse to Patty Bridge Access. The most productive area is the 5 miles from the Powerhouse downstream to Reliance. The upper section of the Hiwassee is located in the Cherokee National Forest and the entire river is designated a Tennessee Scenic River and<span class="sm-text-color-ff00E2">&nbsp;</span><a href="http://www.hiwasseeblueway.com/" rel="noopener noreferrer" target="_blank" class="sm-text-color-ff00E2"><strong>Hiwassee Blueway</strong></a>. Elevated water temperatures in the summer prevent many trout from holding over, but TWRA supplements the Delayed Harvest Area with quality Brown and Rainbow trout in the fall and winter. Hiwassee River receives nearly 75,000 Rainbow, Brown, and Brook trout combined yearly. Public access is excellent for bank, wade, or boat fishing. There are 6 developed access areas with boat ramps. There are numerous parking areas along the river from Hwy. 411 to the Powerhouse that provide easy access and the John Muir National Scenic Trail parallels the river from Childers Creek Access Area to the Powerhouse.

## node n-Q2FuhI

The South Holston River below Boone Dam is Tennessee’s smallest year round trout fishing tailwater. Located about 30 minutes outside of Kingsport, not many folks know about Boone Tailwater which, like its neighbor downstream (Fort Patrick Henry tailwater), produces some of the largest holdover Rainbow Trout across the state. Boone Tailwater supports a relatively small trout population with Rainbow Trout as the predominant species, although Brown Trout make up an important part of this fishery as well. Most trout (either species) range from 9 to 16 inches. However, Boone Tailwater consistently produces both Rainbows and Browns over 22 inches, making this a great spot to catch a “trophy” fish. In fact, the previous state record Rainbow Trout (over 16 pounds!) came from the Boone Tailwater. Brook Trout have also shown the ability to grow to larger sizes, with one over 4 pounds collected during an annual monitoring sample! The large, well-conditioned trout are likely the result of abundant shad that come through the dam, providing the supplemental food resource necessary for exceptional growth. Unfortunately, access along the Boone Tailwater is very limited and is best fished from a boat from the dam down to first bridge. The best time to fish the tailwater from a boat is when they are running 1 generator. Launch at the Smith Shoals Boat Ramp off of Beulah Church Drive and make the 2 mile run upstream.&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;

## node n-oROWU2

The Caney Fork River, located below the Center Hill Dam offers some of the best trout fishing in middle Tennessee. Located in Smith County near the town of Lancaster, the Caney Fork flows 26 miles before running into the Cumberland River in the town of South Carthage. The Center Hill Tailwater provides trout fishing opportunities for about 15 miles from the dam down to the stonewall access. However, the best year round fishing is in the upper 9 miles of the river. Although the water is cold enough to support trout year round, there isn’t any natural reproduction in the river so the TWRA stocks Brown, Brook, and Rainbow trout throughout the year to provide a good place to fish. Many fish will hold over to the next year so the river supports a good population of large Brown Trout and currently holds the State Record for Brook Trout. Public access is excellent for bank, wade, or boat fishing.

## node n-SHzRgv

<span class="sm-text-color-ffffff"><strong>Biologist Sampling Report (2026): </strong></span>The orifice came back online in June of 2024, and we have seen improvements to the fishery. We are still below 2010 numbers, but our trajectory has been positive. Going into summer 2025, we had the highest abundance of trout since 2014. However, things took a turn during summer 2025 as water quality declined. Wet springs are bad for fall water quality in tailwaters. We recorded some of the worst oxygen and temperature conditions in several years during summer and fall of 2025. It’s important to note our 2026 survey was double the Rainbow and Brown Trout average from the tough years of 2020-2023. In early 2026, our overwinter abundance for both Rainbow and Brown Trout was down around 30% compared to early 2025. However, our large fish fared better than the smaller ones. We saw a 3X increase in slot-sized Rainbow Trout despite that overall decline. Our abundance of Brown Trout over 16 inches increased nearly 20% over 2025. There is a strong cohort of 16-20” Brown Trout in the river this year. Our surveys indicate the highest relative abundance for both species from the Dam to Betty’s Island. Our Brown Trout grow about 4 inches each year, so it takes a long time to build the fishery we have today. Be sure to safely handle and quickly return to the water any fish you do not plan to keep.

## node n-CTnirE

The Cherokee Tailwater on the Holston River is a somewhat overlooked trout fishery in east Tennessee as it sits in the shadows of the South Holston, Watauga and Clinch rivers. Located just out of Knoxville, the Holston River below Cherokee Dam provides trout fishing opportunities for approximately 20 miles. Water temperatures in late summer and early fall often get too warm to support trout in the majority of the tailwater, but some fish do find enough coldwater to hold over, so Rainbow and Brown Trout occasionally reach and exceed 20”. Most of the trout range from 11 to 14 inches. There is an abundant food supply in this tailwater, particularly caddis and mayflies, so trout do grow extremely well. Unfortunately, public access is limited to only a few locations on the Cherokee Tailwater and the river is best-suited for fishing from a boat unless you have access through private land. Ramps just below the dam and all the way downstream at Nances Ferry provide the best access for anglers wanting to launch a boat or kayak. The Nances Ferry access also provides wading opportunities several hundred yards upstream when water levels are low. As with all of Tennessee’s tailwaters, water levels are dependent on whether or not the turbines at the dam are operating. When they are, water levels are too high to wade so you will be limited to bank fishing or fishing from a boat.

## node n-lOBQXx

The Obey River, located below the Dale Hollow Dam offers fantastic year round trout fishing. Located in Clay County, the Obey flows 7.5 miles before running into the Cumberland River in the town of Celina. Although trout can be found throughout Dale Hollow Tailwater, the majority of trout fishing is concentrated from Dale Hollow Dam downstream to Moody’s Bend Boat Ramp. The water is cold enough to support trout year round and some trout hold over to reach larger sizes. Natural reproduction has not been documented in the river so the TWRA partners with Dale Hollow National Fish Hatchery to stock nearly 65,000 Brown and Rainbow trout throughout the year to provide trout fishing opportunities. Dale Hollow Hatchery also regularly stocks Hatchery Creek that flows into the Obey River near the hatchery. Public access is excellent for bank, wade, or boat fishing.

## node n-UZZmg7

Not many folks know of the Fort Patrick Henry Tailwater, located just outside of Kingsport, yet it produces some of the largest holdover Rainbow Trout across the state. Relatively short in length and similar to Boone Tailwater, Fort Pat supports a relatively small trout population with Rainbow Trout as the predominant species in the fishery. Most fish range from10 to 18 inches, but no other tailwater has produced more Rainbows over 22 inches (during annual monitoring) than the Fort Patrick Henry Tailwater, making this the perfect place to catch a “trophy” fish. The large, well-conditioned trout produced here are likely the result of abundant shad that come through the dam, providing the supplemental food resource necessary for exceptional growth. Public angling opportunities are essentially confined from Highway 36 down to the boat ramp on the Wesley Road corridor, including some city-owned property in the first bend of the river. The river can be waded here when the generators at the dam are not running, but it becomes extremely dangerous and will rise almost immediately when the generators come on. If fishing from a boat, you will want to fish when they are running 1 generator.

## node n-uhmiDO

This Clinch River has become one of the premier trout fishing destinations in Tennessee. Located just north of Knoxville, the Norris Tailwater provides approximately 14 miles of trout water from the dam down to the Highway 61 Bridge in the town of Clinton. Boat ramps at Miller’s Island, Peach Orchard, and at the Highway 61 Bridge in Clinton provide good access for those wishing to float. There is also a canoe launch that provides access to the 1.5-mile river reach upstream of the weir dam. Wade and bank anglers can access the river just about anywhere between the weir dam and Norris Dam, in the Miller’s Island area, and at Llewelyn Island on the lower end of the tailwater (behind Second Baptist Church). Although there is some documented natural reproduction in the Clinch, TWRA annually stocks Brook, Brown, and Rainbow trout to support the fishery. Many fish will hold over to the next year so the river supports good populations of large Brown Trout and Rainbow Trout. It is not uncommon to catch several Rainbow Trout a day that fall within the 14 to 20 inch protected length range.
